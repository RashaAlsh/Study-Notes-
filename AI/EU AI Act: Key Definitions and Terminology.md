EU AI Act: Key Definitions and Terminology

Overview

Before exploring the EU AI Act’s risk categories, it is important to understand the key terms used throughout the legislation. These definitions establish a common vocabulary for governments, businesses, developers, and AI users, helping clarify responsibilities and compliance requirements.

1. Artificial Intelligence System

An AI system is a machine-based system that operates with varying levels of autonomy and may adapt after deployment. It infers from its inputs how to generate outputs such as predictions, recommendations, content, or decisions that can influence physical or virtual environments.

Examples include:

* Credit-scoring systems.
* AI-powered chatbots.
* Autonomous driving systems.
* Predictive analytics tools.

2. Provider

A provider is an individual or organization that develops an AI system, or has one developed, and places it on the market or puts it into service under its own name or trademark.

Example: A company that develops and markets an AI-powered medical diagnostic tool.

3. Deployer

A deployer is an individual or organization that uses an AI system under its authority in a professional context, except where the use is part of a personal, non-professional activity.

Example: A hospital that uses an AI diagnostic tool to support medical professionals.

4. High-Risk AI System

A high-risk AI system is an AI system classified as high risk under the EU AI Act because it falls within specified categories or presents particular risks to health, safety, or fundamental rights.

Examples may include AI systems used in:

* Recruitment and employment decisions.
* Education and vocational training.
* Access to essential services.
* Certain medical and safety-related applications.

High-risk AI systems are subject to additional requirements, including risk management, documentation, human oversight, and monitoring, as applicable.

5. Conformity Assessment

A conformity assessment is the process of demonstrating that an AI system meets the applicable requirements of the EU AI Act.

Depending on the system, this process may involve internal checks, technical documentation, quality management procedures, or assessment by a notified body.

6. CE Marking

CE marking indicates that a product complies with applicable EU legislation requiring the marking.

For AI systems covered by the relevant conformity requirements, CE marking indicates that the applicable conformity procedures have been completed. It is not required for every AI system.

7. Fundamental Rights

Fundamental rights are the basic rights and freedoms protected under EU law, including the EU Charter of Fundamental Rights.

Relevant examples include:

* Privacy and protection of personal data.
* Non-discrimination and equality.
* Human dignity.
* Freedom of expression.
* The right to an effective remedy.

The EU AI Act aims to ensure that AI systems are developed and used in ways that respect these rights.

Key Takeaway

Understanding these definitions is essential for determining who is responsible for an AI system and which legal obligations apply.
