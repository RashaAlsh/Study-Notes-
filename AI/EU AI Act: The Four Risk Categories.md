EU AI Act: The Four Risk Categories

Overview

The EU AI Act follows a risk-based approach: AI systems are regulated according to the potential harm they may cause to people’s health, safety, and fundamental rights.

Rather than applying the same rules to every AI system, the Act establishes different levels of regulation based on risk.

1. Unacceptable Risk — Prohibited AI

These AI practices are considered to pose unacceptable risks and are prohibited under the EU AI Act, subject to the specific legal provisions and exceptions.

Examples include:

* Social scoring: Certain systems that evaluate or classify people based on social behavior in ways that lead to prohibited harmful treatment.
* Harmful manipulation: AI that uses prohibited manipulative or deceptive techniques, or exploits vulnerabilities, causing or likely to cause significant harm.
* Certain biometric surveillance: Specific uses of real-time remote biometric identification in publicly accessible spaces for law enforcement, which are generally prohibited subject to narrow exceptions.

Key principle: Prohibited AI practices cannot be used where the Act bans them.

2. High Risk — Strictly Regulated AI

High-risk AI systems can significantly affect people’s safety, health, or fundamental rights. They are permitted but must meet specific compliance requirements.

Examples include:

* Critical infrastructure: Certain AI safety components used in energy, transport, and other essential infrastructure.
* Healthcare: AI systems used in regulated medical devices or other qualifying high-risk medical applications.
* Education: Certain systems used to evaluate examinations, assess learning outcomes, or determine access to education.
* Employment: AI used to screen applicants, evaluate candidates, or make certain employment-related decisions.
* Law enforcement and border control: Certain AI applications used in these contexts.

Depending on the system, requirements may include risk management, data governance, technical documentation, human oversight, and conformity assessment.

Key principle: High-risk AI is allowed only when the applicable legal requirements are met.

3. Limited Risk — Transparency Requirements

Certain AI systems trigger specific transparency obligations, helping people understand when they are interacting with AI or viewing AI-generated content.

Examples include:

* Chatbots: People must be informed that they are interacting with AI where the applicable rules require it.
* Deepfakes: Certain AI-generated or manipulated images, audio, and video must be disclosed as artificially generated or manipulated.
* Emotion recognition: People exposed to certain emotion-recognition systems must be informed of their use, subject to applicable rules.

Not every system commonly described as limited risk has the same obligations. Requirements depend on the relevant provisions of the Act.

Key principle: Transparency helps people make informed decisions about AI interactions and content.

4. Minimal Risk — Few Additional Restrictions

Many everyday AI applications fall outside the Act’s high-risk and prohibited categories and are subject to few or no additional requirements under the AI Act.

Examples include:

* Spam filters.
* AI opponents in video games.
* Certain product recommendation systems.
* Basic AI-powered sorting and filtering tools.

Organizations must still comply with other applicable laws, and some voluntary codes of conduct may apply.

Key principle: Lower-risk AI can generally be developed and used with fewer regulatory burdens.

Why the Risk-Based Approach Matters

The EU AI Act aims to balance innovation with the protection of individuals and society.

* Higher risk means stronger safeguards: Systems that can significantly affect people’s lives face stricter requirements.
* Transparency builds trust: People should understand when certain AI technologies are being used.
* Lower-risk innovation remains possible: Everyday AI applications can generally be developed without the same regulatory burden as high-risk systems.

Key Takeaway

The four risk categories form the foundation of the EU AI Act:

* Unacceptable risk: Prohibited AI practices.
* High risk: AI subject to strict requirements.
* Limited risk: Specific transparency obligations.
* Minimal risk: Generally few additional regulatory requirements.

Correctly classifying an AI system is essential for understanding the obligations that apply to it.
