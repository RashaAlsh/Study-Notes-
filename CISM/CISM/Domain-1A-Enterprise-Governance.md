CISM Domain 1A: Enterprise Governance

1. Overview

CISM Domain 1 focuses on Information Security Governance.

Part A, Enterprise Governance, explains how information security should be directed, controlled, and integrated into the organization’s overall business strategy.

The objective is to ensure that security supports business goals, protects information assets, meets compliance obligations, and operates within the organization’s risk tolerance.

Domain 1A contains three main sections:

1. 1A1: Organizational Culture
2. 1A2: Legal, Regulatory, and Contractual Requirements
3. 1A3: Organizational Structures, Roles, and Responsibilities

The Most Important CISM Principle

It’s all about the business!

Information security should not operate as an isolated IT function. It must support the organization’s mission, objectives, priorities, and acceptable level of risk.

For example, a bank needs strong controls to protect financial transactions and customer information. A hospital needs to protect patient information while ensuring that critical medical systems remain available.

Security decisions should therefore reflect business requirements rather than focus only on technical protection.

2. Important Governance Concepts

Before studying the individual sections, understand the difference between governance, strategy, and management.

Governance vs. Strategy vs. Management

Concept	Main Question	Explanation
Governance	Why and what?	Establishes direction, oversight, policies, priorities, and risk appetite
Strategy	How, at a high level?	Defines the roadmap for achieving the desired security state
Management	How do we execute?	Implements policies, operates controls, and manages daily activities

Example:

Imagine an organization wants to improve protection of customer information.

* Governance: Leadership establishes the requirement to protect customer information and defines acceptable risk.
* Strategy: The security strategy identifies priorities such as access management, encryption, and monitoring.
* Management: Security and IT teams implement access controls, encryption, and monitoring procedures.

Enterprise Governance

Enterprise governance is the overall framework of rules, policies, standards, and procedures used to direct, monitor, and control organizational activities.

It covers:

* Organizational mission and vision.
* Business objectives.
* Leadership and decision-making.
* Organizational structure.
* Risk management.
* Performance monitoring.
* Compliance and accountability.

Its purpose is to ensure that business risks and security risks are managed cohesively.

Information Security Governance

Information security governance is a specialized part of enterprise governance focused on protecting the organization’s information assets.

It ensures that:

* Security supports business objectives.
* Security policies are integrated into business processes.
* Responsibilities are clearly defined.
* Risks are understood and managed.
* Security investments deliver value.
* Performance and compliance are monitored.

Key principle: Information security governance uses the organization’s existing governance structure to embed security throughout the business.

Planning Horizons

The handout distinguishes three planning levels:

Planning Level	Typical Time Horizon	Focus	Example
Strategic	3–5 years	Long-term organizational direction	Enterprise security strategy
Tactical	Approximately 1 year	Turning strategy into initiatives and plans	Annual security program plan
Operational	1–3 months	Execution of specific activities	Patching, monitoring, and access reviews

These timeframes are illustrative planning horizons, not universal requirements.

3. 1A1 — Organizational Culture

Organizational culture is the way employees work, interact, make decisions, and behave.

Security is not only about technology. It also depends on people’s attitudes, actions, and understanding of their responsibilities.

Accountability vs. Responsibility

This is one of the most important CISM exam concepts.

Concept	Meaning	Can It Be Delegated?
Accountability	The obligation to answer for decisions, actions, or results	No
Responsibility	The duty to perform a task or achieve an objective	Yes

Example:

A manager assigns an employee responsibility for implementing a security control.

* The employee is responsible for completing the task.
* The manager remains accountable for the overall outcome.

Remember:

* Accountability generally belongs to one person or entity.
* Multiple people may share responsibility.
* Delegating a task does not remove the delegator’s accountability.

Span of Control

Span of control refers to the number of employees who report directly to a manager.

The handout suggests that fewer than ten direct reports is often considered manageable.

The appropriate span depends on:

* Complexity of the work.
* Experience of employees.
* Management capabilities.
* Organizational structure.
* Degree of supervision required.

Information Security Governance

Information security governance is a top-down activity that helps management understand and control the security program.

It connects business objectives with:

* Security policies.
* Security controls.
* Risk management.
* Security initiatives.
* Performance metrics.

Most important objective:

Ensure that the information security strategy aligns with the strategic goals and objectives of the business.

Why Top-Down Governance Matters

Executive support is essential because leadership provides:

* Authority.
* Budget.
* Resources.
* Strategic direction.
* Organizational influence.
* Accountability.

Without management support, security initiatives may struggle to receive funding, cooperation, or organizational priority.

Alignment with Business Objectives

Business alignment means integrating information security into the organization’s overall activities.

Two areas must be understood.

1. Organizational Foundation

* Mission: Why does the organization exist?
* Goals and objectives: What does the organization need to achieve?
* Strategic approach: What activities will help achieve those goals?

2. Key Business Factors

* Organizational culture.
* Value of business assets.
* Competitive position.
* Legal requirements.
* Regulatory requirements.
* Business priorities.

Example:

An online retailer may prioritize website availability, customer data protection, and payment security.

Its security strategy should reflect these priorities rather than implementing controls without considering business needs.

Security Governance Principles

A security governance program should connect four elements.

Element	Meaning	Key Question
Vision	Desired future state of security	What do we aspire to achieve?
Mission	Core purpose of the security function	Why does the security function exist?
Strategic Objectives	Specific goals that support the vision	What must we accomplish?
Implementation and KPIs	Actions and measurements used to achieve and evaluate objectives	What must we do, and how do we measure success?

Example:

* Vision: Establish a trusted and secure digital environment.
* Mission: Protect organizational information while supporting business operations.
* Strategic objective: Reduce unauthorized access to critical systems.
* Implementation: Introduce stronger authentication and access reviews.
* KPI: Measure the percentage of critical systems protected by the required authentication controls.

ISACA Code of Professional Ethics

CISM professionals are expected to demonstrate ethical and professional conduct.

The handout highlights seven key principles:

1. Support the implementation of and encourage compliance with appropriate standards and procedures.
2. Perform duties with due diligence and professional care, in accordance with professional standards.
3. Serve stakeholders lawfully and honestly while maintaining high standards of conduct and character.
4. Maintain the privacy and confidentiality of information obtained during professional duties, unless disclosure is legally required.
5. Maintain professional competence and undertake only work that can be completed competently.
6. Inform appropriate parties of work results and disclose all significant facts known to them.
7. Support professional education to improve stakeholder understanding of security governance and management.

Exam tip: Professional ethics means acting honestly, protecting confidential information, maintaining competence, and communicating significant findings appropriately.

Importance of Information Security Governance

Good information security governance provides several benefits.

Benefit	Explanation
Reduces Liability	Helps protect the organization and leadership from legal and civil consequences
Ensures Compliance	Supports adherence to applicable legal, regulatory, and organizational requirements
Manages Risk	Identifies and reduces risks to acceptable levels
Optimizes Resources	Helps allocate security resources efficiently

Six Outcomes of Information Security Governance

These are particularly important for exam preparation.

Outcome	Explanation
1. Strategic Alignment	Security investments and activities support business objectives
2. Risk Management	Risks are understood, monitored, and maintained within organizational risk tolerance
3. Value Delivery	Security investments provide measurable business value and cost-effective protection
4. Resource Optimization	People, technology, knowledge, and financial resources are used efficiently
5. Performance Measurement	Metrics, audits, and assessments measure whether objectives are achieved
6. Assurance Process Integration	Security assurance activities are coordinated across the organization

Assurance Processes

Assurance processes provide stakeholders with confidence that security controls and policies:

* Are properly implemented.
* Operate as intended.
* Support organizational risk management.
* Meet compliance objectives.

Examples include:

* Risk assessments.
* Security audits.
* Vulnerability scans.
* Penetration testing.
* Compliance reviews.

Important: Assurance is about providing evidence that controls are working as intended.

Governance Charter vs. Scope

Concept	Meaning
Charter	Defines authority, objectives, responsibilities, and the reason the security program exists
Scope	Defines the boundaries and coverage of the security program

Example:

A security charter establishes the authority and objectives of the information security program.

Its scope specifies which business units, employees, systems, locations, and information assets are covered.

Information Security vs. IT Security vs. Cybersecurity

These terms are related but have different scopes.

Concept	Coverage	Example
Information Security	Protects information in every format and location	Paper records, databases, printed documents
IT Security	Protects information and resources within IT systems	Servers, applications, databases
Cybersecurity	Focuses on protecting connected systems and digital environments from cyber threats	Network attacks, ransomware, phishing

A useful way to remember the relationship:

* Information Security is the broadest concept.
* IT Security is a subset of Information Security.
* Cybersecurity is a related discipline focused on cyber threats and connected environments.

Organizational Culture and Security Awareness

A positive security culture develops over time and requires leadership support.

Key influences include:

* Transparency and accountability.
* Formal and informal organizational structures.
* Employee attitudes and behavior.
* Teamwork.
* Individual backgrounds and work ethics.

Security Manager’s Role

The security manager needs both technical and interpersonal skills.

Responsibilities include:

* Building relationships across the organization.
* Influencing employee behavior.
* Communicating security requirements in understandable language.
* Addressing employee concerns.
* Encouraging security awareness.
* Integrating security into business activities.

The manager should communicate the personal and business value of security.

A useful question is:

“What’s in it for me?”

Employees are more likely to support security practices when they understand how those practices relate to their work.

Indicators of a Positive Security Culture

* Security is considered early in projects.
* Employees understand their security responsibilities.
* Employees know how to report incidents.
* Security is part of normal business activities.
* Management supports security initiatives.
* Employees understand the importance of protecting information.

Exam tip: Developing a security-aware culture takes time. The security manager must build relationships, influence behavior, and obtain leadership support.

Acceptable Use Policy (AUP)

An Acceptable Use Policy defines permitted and prohibited activities involving organizational information systems and data.

It generally applies to:

* Employees.
* Contractors.
* Consultants.
* Third parties.
* Other individuals using organizational information assets.

Typical AUP topics include:

* Acceptable system usage.
* Prohibited activities.
* Access control.
* Data classification and handling.
* Incident reporting.
* Information disclosure.
* Mobile device usage.
* Consequences of policy violations.

Recipients typically acknowledge their agreement to follow the policy.

Ethics and Ethical Behavior

Ethics means behaving in a manner that respects laws, regulations, and the rights and welfare of others.

Ethical conduct supports trust in the organization’s security practices.

Keys to success:

* Leadership should demonstrate ethical behavior.
* Policies should reflect organizational values.
* Ethical guidelines should align with the mission and vision.
* Employees should understand expected professional conduct.

Exam tip: Leadership must model the behavior it expects from employees.

4. 1A2 — Legal, Regulatory, and Contractual Requirements

Organizations must understand and comply with the requirements that govern their operations.

Compliance is a major driver of information security governance.

Compliance

Compliance means adhering to applicable:

* Laws.
* Regulations.
* Standards.
* Contractual obligations.
* Organizational requirements.

Organizations implement policies, procedures, and controls to meet these requirements.

Types of Compliance Requirements

Type	Description	Examples from the Handout
Legal	Requirements imposed by laws passed by government bodies	CCPA
Regulatory	Requirements imposed or enforced by regulatory authorities	HIPAA, FERPA, SOX, GDPR
Contractual	Requirements established through legally binding agreements	Vendor and customer agreements requiring particular security standards

Legal Requirements

Legal requirements are established through legislation.

They generally apply to organizations operating within the relevant jurisdiction or falling within the law’s scope.

Regulatory Requirements

Regulatory requirements are established or enforced by regulatory bodies and agencies.

Noncompliance may lead to:

* Regulatory investigations.
* Audits.
* Financial penalties.
* Enforcement actions.
* Other legal consequences.

Contractual Requirements

Contractual requirements arise from agreements between organizations.

Examples include:

* Vendor security requirements.
* Customer data protection obligations.
* Security assessment requirements.
* Incident notification deadlines.
* Requirements to follow particular standards.

Important: Contractual requirements may be legally enforceable even when they are not directly imposed by legislation.

Due Diligence

Due diligence means taking reasonable steps to identify, assess, and address organizational risks.

In information security, it involves proactively examining systems, networks, processes, and information to identify weaknesses and implement appropriate safeguards.

Examples include:

* Auditing IT infrastructure.
* Testing incident response plans.
* Conducting employee awareness training.
* Performing security assessments.
* Reviewing vulnerabilities.

Remember:

Due diligence is not simply writing policies. It requires taking reasonable, demonstrable actions to understand and manage risk.

Identifying Applicable Compliance Standards

Organizations must determine which requirements apply to their operations.

Important considerations include:

1. Industry and Geographic Location

Different industries and jurisdictions have different requirements.

2. Data Processing Activities

Organizations handling personal information or protected health information may have additional obligations.

3. Customer Contracts and Agreements

Contracts may require compliance with specific security standards, such as ISO 27001.

4. Legal and Compliance Experts

Consult internal or external legal and compliance specialists to interpret obligations.

5. Compliance Frameworks

Use relevant frameworks to help structure and implement security practices.

6. Regulatory Changes

Monitor changes in laws and regulations because compliance obligations can change over time.

7. Regular Compliance Assessments

Conduct gap analyses and risk assessments to identify areas where the organization is not meeting requirements.

Supporting Compliance Activities

A strong compliance program should:

* Document the compliance program.
* Train employees.
* Implement appropriate security controls.
* Monitor activities.
* Conduct audits and assessments.
* Respond to incidents promptly.
* Review requirements regularly.

Business Record Retention

Organizations must manage business records throughout their lifecycle.

This includes:

* Creation.
* Storage.
* Retrieval.
* Retention.
* Disposal.

Records may include:

* Emails.
* Financial documents.
* Employee records.
* Business communications.
* Other organizational information.

Record Retention Considerations

Retention requirements depend on:

* Applicable laws.
* Regulatory requirements.
* Geographic jurisdiction.
* Business needs.
* Risk management considerations.
* Pending litigation.

Organizations must know:

* How long records must be retained.
* Where records must be stored.
* Who can access them.
* Under what conditions they may be destroyed.
* When they must be produced or released.

Legal Hold

A legal hold is an instruction to preserve relevant records and information because they may be required for litigation, investigation, or another legal matter.

Once a legal hold applies, normal disposal procedures must not destroy the relevant information.

Exam tip: Legal and regulatory requirements are high-priority compliance drivers. The organization cannot simply ignore mandatory obligations because another investment appears more beneficial.

5. 1A3 — Organizational Structures, Roles, and Responsibilities

Effective information security governance requires clearly defined organizational roles and responsibilities.

Employees and management must understand:

* Who makes decisions.
* Who performs tasks.
* Who owns risks.
* Who implements controls.
* Who provides oversight.
* Who must be informed.

Roles vs. Responsibilities

Concept	Meaning	Example
Role	A position or function within the organization	Project Manager, Developer, CISO
Responsibility	Specific duties associated with a role	Developing, testing, or maintaining software

Job titles may vary between organizations. Focus on the function and authority of the role rather than the exact title.

Board of Directors

The Board of Directors is generally the highest governing body of an organization.

Its responsibilities may include:

* Providing strategic direction.
* Selecting and overseeing the CEO.
* Setting or approving risk appetite.
* Establishing oversight committees.
* Approving major organizational decisions.
* Monitoring organizational performance.
* Exercising governance oversight.

The board provides direction and oversight rather than managing daily security operations.

Senior Management

Senior management translates the board’s direction into organizational execution.

Responsibilities include:

* Implementing board directives.
* Managing organizational assets.
* Allocating budgets and personnel.
* Establishing governance functions.
* Overseeing risk management.
* Supporting information security initiatives.
* Ensuring organizational policies are implemented.

Senior management is critical because it provides the resources and authority needed to execute security strategy.

Chief Executive Officer (CEO)

The CEO is the highest-ranking executive responsible for the overall success of the organization.

The CEO generally:

* Leads organizational operations.
* Implements the board’s strategic direction.
* Oversees senior management.
* Ensures business objectives are pursued.
* Supports organizational governance.

Chief Information Security Officer (CISO)

The CISO is responsible for leading the information security program and strategy.

Typical responsibilities include:

* Developing information security strategy.
* Developing and maintaining security policies.
* Managing the information security program.
* Coordinating security risk management.
* Supporting security control implementation.
* Overseeing security operations.
* Reporting security performance and risks.
* Ensuring security aligns with business goals.

Important exam point: The handout identifies the CISO as responsible for enforcing the organization’s information security policies.

Chief Privacy Officer (CPO)

The CPO is responsible for the organization’s privacy program.

Typical responsibilities include:

* Protecting sensitive personal information.
* Overseeing personally identifiable information (PII) practices.
* Supporting consumer privacy.
* Coordinating privacy compliance.
* Working with the CISO on information protection.

Chief Risk Officer (CRO)

The CRO oversees enterprise risk management.

Responsibilities may include:

* Identifying organizational risks.
* Evaluating business risks.
* Coordinating risk management activities.
* Supporting risk mitigation.
* Overseeing financial and operational risk.
* Including information security risks within the broader enterprise risk picture.

The CRO helps ensure security risks are considered as part of enterprise-wide risk management.

Risk Management Roles

The handout identifies two important functions.

Role	Responsibility
Risk Owner	Formally accountable for managing a particular risk within their area
Control Owner	Responsible for deploying and maintaining controls that address risks

These responsibilities may exist within existing organizational job titles rather than requiring separate positions.

Example:

A business manager may be the risk owner for a business process, while an IT manager may own the technical controls used to protect it.

Business Process Owners

Business process owners are management-level personnel responsible for business processes.

They may not be technical experts, but they understand:

* Business requirements.
* Operational priorities.
* Process dependencies.
* Business impact.
* Required service levels.

Their responsibilities may include:

* Defining access requirements.
* Managing business assets.
* Leading business processes.
* Supporting access management.
* Participating in risk assessments.
* Providing input into security decisions.
* Supporting internal control and audit activities.

Important: Business process owners understand what the business needs. IT and security teams provide technical expertise to help meet those needs.

Security Steering Committee

A security steering committee is a group of stakeholders from different organizational areas.

Its purpose is to ensure that business functions affected by information security are represented in security decisions.

Typical responsibilities include:

* Providing a communication channel between security and business teams.
* Reviewing security initiatives.
* Coordinating priorities.
* Supporting resource decisions.
* Addressing conflicts between business and security requirements.
* Promoting alignment between security and organizational goals.

Exam tip: A governance committee is particularly useful when the question asks how to improve alignment between information security and business functions.

RACI Matrix

A RACI matrix defines roles and responsibilities for tasks, projects, and processes.

RACI stands for:

Letter	Meaning	Explanation
R	Responsible	Performs the actual work; there may be multiple responsible parties
A	Accountable	Has ultimate responsibility for the outcome; normally one accountable party
C	Consulted	Provides advice, expertise, and input
I	Informed	Receives updates but does not provide direct input

Understanding RACI with an Example

Imagine an organization is implementing a new security control.

* Responsible: IT staff configure and implement the control.
* Accountable: The designated manager owns the final outcome.
* Consulted: Legal and business stakeholders provide input.
* Informed: Senior management receives progress updates.

Sample RACI Matrix

Task	CISO	Incident Response Team	Legal	CEO
Incident Response Management	A	R	C	I
Technical Investigation	R	A	C	I
Legal Compliance	I	R	A	C
Remediation Planning	A	R	C	I

This is an illustrative matrix from the handout. Actual assignments depend on the organization’s governance structure and defined authority.

Keys to Successful RACI Implementation

1. Define roles and responsibilities clearly. Everyone should understand their duties and authority.
2. Identify key tasks and deliverables. Break larger activities into manageable tasks.
3. Assign RACI responsibilities. Clearly identify who is responsible, accountable, consulted, and informed.
4. Consider skills and expertise. Assign work to people with appropriate knowledge and capabilities.
5. Communicate and review. Share the matrix and update it when responsibilities change.

A RACI matrix should be a living document.

Most Important RACI Exam Rule

Responsibility can be delegated, but accountability cannot.

There may be multiple Responsible parties, but normally only one Accountable party for a particular task or deliverable.

7. CISM Exam Mindset

Domain 1A questions frequently test whether you understand that information security exists to support the business.

High-Value Exam Principles

1. Business alignment is the priority. Security must support organizational goals and objectives.
2. Governance is top-down. Executive leadership provides direction, authority, and support.
3. Governance is not management. Governance sets direction; management executes it.
4. Accountability cannot be delegated. Responsibility can be assigned to others.
5. Security culture takes time. It requires leadership support, communication, and employee engagement.
6. Security managers need interpersonal skills. Influencing behavior is as important as technical knowledge.
7. Use a governance committee for alignment. Cross-functional committees encourage communication and business participation.
8. Compliance is a major driver. Mandatory legal and regulatory requirements must be addressed.
9. Due diligence requires action. Organizations must demonstrate reasonable efforts to identify and manage risks.
10. Preserve records when required. Legal holds prevent destruction of relevant information.
11. Know organizational roles. Understand the board, CEO, CISO, CPO, CRO, and business process owners.
12. Use RACI to clarify responsibilities. Every task should have clear ownership and communication expectations.

Common Exam Traps

Incorrect Assumption	Correct Understanding
Information security is primarily an IT problem	It is an enterprise governance and business responsibility
The CISO should make all business risk decisions	Business leadership and designated risk owners make risk decisions
Governance and management are the same	Governance directs; management executes
Responsibility and accountability are interchangeable	Responsibility can be delegated; accountability cannot
Security awareness alone creates a security culture	Culture requires leadership, communication, and sustained effort
Security should always prioritize maximum technical protection	Controls should be proportionate to business risk and requirements
A security charter defines the program’s boundaries	The charter defines authority and objectives; scope defines boundaries
Cybersecurity and information security are identical	Information security has a broader scope
Compliance is optional when security investments are expensive	Mandatory legal and regulatory requirements must be addressed
All business records can be deleted after their normal retention period	Legal holds and other obligations may prevent destruction
The CISO owns every organizational risk	Risk ownership belongs to the designated business or risk owner
The security steering committee performs all security operations	It provides coordination, oversight, and business alignment
A RACI task can have several accountable parties	Normally, one party is accountable for each task or deliverable

9. Practice Questions

Use these questions to test your understanding of the handout’s key concepts.

Question 1

Which of the following BEST ensures alignment between information security and business functions?

A. Providing more funding for information security.

B. Establishing a security awareness program.

C. Establishing an information security governance committee.

D. Developing additional information security policies.

Correct answer: C — Establishing an information security governance committee.

Explanation: A cross-functional committee brings together business stakeholders and security leadership. It encourages collaboration, communication, and decisions that align security with business objectives.

Question 2

What is the primary compliance driver that requires no further business justification?

A. Business benefits.

B. Alignment with industry best practices.

C. Business continuity investment.

D. Regulatory compliance.

Correct answer: D — Regulatory compliance.

Explanation: Mandatory regulatory requirements must be met. The organization cannot simply choose not to comply because another investment appears more beneficial.

Question 3

Who is responsible for enforcing the organization’s information security policies according to the handout?

A. Security steering committee.

B. Chief Information Security Officer (CISO).

C. Chief Information Officer (CIO).

D. Chief Risk Officer (CRO).

Correct answer: B — Chief Information Security Officer (CISO).

Explanation: The CISO leads the information security program and is responsible for security policy enforcement in the handout’s organizational model.

10. Final Takeaways

CISM Domain 1A establishes the foundation for the entire CISM certification: information security must be governed as part of the business.

The most important concepts to master are:

* The difference between governance, strategy, and management.
* Alignment between security and business objectives.
* Accountability versus responsibility.
* The six outcomes of information security governance.
* Organizational culture and the security manager’s role.
* Acceptable Use Policies and professional ethics.
* Legal, regulatory, and contractual compliance.
* Due diligence and business record retention.
* The responsibilities of key organizational roles.
* Risk ownership and control ownership.
* RACI matrices and clear responsibility assignments.

Final Exam Principle

Information security governance exists to support the organization, not to operate independently of it.

The board and senior management provide direction and oversight. The CISO leads the information security program. Business and risk owners make decisions within their areas of authority. Security teams implement controls and support business objectives.
