CISM Domain 1B: Information Security Strategy

1. Domain Overview

Information Security Strategy is the second part of CISM Domain 1: Information Security Governance. It focuses on ensuring that information security supports the enterprise’s business objectives and provides a structured direction for the security program.

Domain 1 is divided into two areas:

Area	Main focus
Domain 1A – Enterprise Governance	Organizational culture, legal and regulatory requirements, governance roles, and responsibilities
Domain 1B – Information Security Strategy	Strategy development, governance frameworks and standards, and strategic planning and implementation

The central principle is that information security must enable and protect business objectives rather than operate as an isolated technical function.

Governance remains the responsibility of the board of directors and senior management. Security leaders help translate that governance direction into an actionable information security strategy.

2. Strategy Development and Objectives

2.1 Core Purpose and Business Alignment

An information security strategy defines the direction, priorities, and objectives of the organization’s security program.

Before developing a strategy, the organization should answer one fundamental question:

What is the goal of the strategy and the program it enables?

This question establishes the purpose of the strategy and helps prevent security initiatives from becoming disconnected technical projects.

An effective strategy should:

* Support the enterprise’s mission and business objectives.
* Address the organization’s information security risks.
* Establish priorities for security investments.
* Define the desired future security state.
* Provide direction for people, processes, and technology.
* Establish ways to measure progress and effectiveness.

Exam focus: Business alignment is more important than simply implementing more security technologies. Security investments should be justified by their contribution to business objectives and risk management.

2.2 Six Key Outcomes of an Information Security Strategy

A successful security strategy should deliver six important outcomes.

Outcome	Explanation
Strategic Alignment	Connects security objectives and controls directly to the enterprise’s strategic plan.
Risk Management	Establishes processes to identify, assess, and manage risk within defined tolerance levels.
Value Delivery	Ensures security investments provide measurable business value and support enterprise objectives.
Resource Optimization	Allocates people, budgets, tools, and other resources efficiently.
Performance Measurement	Uses KPIs and KRIs to measure security performance and monitor risk exposure.
Assurance Process Integration	Coordinates audits, compliance activities, and assurance processes without creating isolated organizational functions.

Remember: A security strategy is not successful merely because security controls exist. It must demonstrate alignment, value, risk management, and measurable performance.

2.3 Assumptions and Prerequisites

A sound security strategy depends on the organization understanding what it needs to protect.

Important prerequisites include:

* Identifying information assets.
* Understanding where assets are located.
* Determining the sensitivity and value of information.
* Establishing data classification schemes.
* Implementing structured data lifecycle management.

Without an understanding of its information assets, an organization cannot reliably determine where protection is most important.

For example, an organization should understand which information is public, internal, confidential, or highly sensitive before deciding which access restrictions and protection mechanisms are appropriate.

Exam focus: Asset identification and information classification provide the foundation for meaningful risk assessment and security planning.

2.4 Cognitive Biases in Strategy Development

Strategic decisions can be affected by cognitive biases. These biases may cause decision-makers to underestimate risk, misallocate resources, or resist necessary changes.

Bias	Explanation	Potential consequence
Overconfidence	Believing that predictions and decisions are more accurate than they really are.	Underestimating threats and security risks.
Optimism bias	Assuming that negative events are less likely to happen to the organization.	Insufficient preparation for incidents.
Anchoring bias	Relying too heavily on the first information or estimate received.	Poor decisions based on outdated or incomplete assumptions.
Status quo bias	Preferring existing methods simply because they are familiar.	Resistance to necessary security improvements.
Mental accounting	Treating funds or resources as separate categories without considering the overall enterprise picture.	Inefficient allocation of security budgets.
Herding instinct	Following industry trends or other organizations without independent evaluation.	Adopting controls that may not address actual business risks.
False consensus	Assuming that everyone shares the same opinions or assumptions.	Overlooking disagreement, risks, and vulnerabilities.

Exam tip: Strategy should be based on objective business requirements, evidence, and risk analysis—not assumptions, popularity, or personal preferences.

2.5 Desired State

The desired state describes the organization’s intended future information security condition.

It should provide a complete picture of how security will operate across:

* Principles: Security policies, standards, and guiding requirements.
* Processes: Procedures and operational activities.
* People: Roles, responsibilities, skills, and accountability.
* Technology: Systems, infrastructure, tools, and security controls.

The desired state is the destination. The roadmap describes how the organization will get there.

Two important ISACA-related frameworks discussed in this material are COBIT and BMIS.

2.6 COBIT Framework

COBIT (Control Objectives for Information and Related Technologies) provides a framework for the governance and management of enterprise information and technology.

Its principles include:

* Meeting stakeholder needs and delivering value.
* Taking a holistic approach.
* Adapting to changing circumstances through dynamic governance.
* Distinguishing governance from management.

The distinction between governance and management is particularly important:

Governance	Management
Evaluates stakeholder needs and conditions.	Plans and executes activities.
Sets direction and establishes priorities.	Implements the established direction.
Monitors performance and outcomes.	Operates and manages processes.
Generally exercised by the board and governing bodies.	Generally performed by executive and operational management.

Exam focus: Governance establishes direction and oversight; management executes the direction.

2.7 BMIS Framework

The Business Model for Information Security (BMIS) is a holistic model that considers the relationship between business operations and information security.

It contains four core elements:

Element	Explanation
Organizational Design and Strategy	The organization’s structure, business direction, and strategic objectives.
People	Individuals, skills, behavior, culture, and responsibilities.
Process	Business and security processes, procedures, and workflows.
Technology	Systems, infrastructure, applications, and technical capabilities.

These elements are balanced by six dynamic interrelated forces, described in the material as:

1. Governance
2. Culture
3. Enabling and Support
4. Emergence
5. Human Factors
6. Architecture

BMIS emphasizes that information security is not simply a technology problem. It is influenced by organizational behavior, culture, processes, governance, and technology.

COBIT vs. BMIS

Framework	Main emphasis
COBIT	Governance and management of enterprise information and technology
BMIS	Holistic relationships between business and information security

2.8 Elements of an Information Security Strategy

A strategy should contain three important elements:

Element	Meaning	Example
Roadmap	The sequence of initiatives and projects needed to move from the current state to the desired state.	Implement identity governance before expanding privileged access monitoring.
Resources	The capabilities available to execute the strategy.	Budget, employees, skills, tools, and organizational structures.
Constraints	Factors that limit or influence implementation.	Budget restrictions, organizational culture, risk appetite, and regulatory obligations.

The roadmap should address both short-term and long-term initiatives.

Remember:

* Desired state = Where the organization wants to be.
* Roadmap = How it will get there.
* Resources = What it has available.
* Constraints = What limits its options.

3. Governance Frameworks and Standards

3.1 Balanced Scorecard

The Balanced Scorecard is a performance management approach that measures organizational performance from four perspectives.

It helps organizations evaluate whether activities are contributing to broader strategic objectives.

Perspective	Focus	Example
Financial	Shareholder value, profitability, and financial performance.	Security investment costs and financial benefits.
Customer	Customer satisfaction, trust, and retention.	Customer confidence in information protection.
Internal Business Process	Efficiency and effectiveness of internal operations.	Incident response efficiency and access review processes.
Learning and Growth	Employee skills, training, innovation, and organizational culture.	Security awareness and professional development.

A useful way to remember the four perspectives is to think of a vehicle dashboard: no single indicator provides the complete picture of performance.

Exam focus: The Balanced Scorecard measures performance from multiple perspectives. It is not limited to financial performance.

3.2 Enterprise Information Security Architecture (EISA)

Enterprise Information Security Architecture (EISA) provides a structured blueprint for integrating security requirements into enterprise architecture.

Its purpose is to ensure that security capabilities support business requirements and are consistently incorporated into systems and processes.

Architecture frameworks mentioned in the material include:

* TOGAF: The Open Group Architecture Framework.
* Zachman Framework: A framework for organizing and describing enterprise architecture.

EISA helps connect:

* Business objectives.
* Information and technology architecture.
* Security requirements.
* Security controls.
* Enterprise processes.

For example, if an organization introduces a new cloud-based business platform, its architecture should account for identity management, data protection, access controls, and monitoring requirements.

Exam focus: Enterprise security architecture connects business requirements to security controls through a structured design.

3.3 Enterprise Risk Management (ERM)

Enterprise Risk Management provides an organization-wide approach to identifying, assessing, and managing risk.

Frameworks mentioned in the training material include:

* COSO ERM Framework
* ISO 31000:2018

These frameworks support a consistent approach to risk management and help establish a common risk language across the organization.

ERM is broader than information security risk management because it considers risks affecting the enterprise as a whole.

Information security risk should therefore be considered within the organization’s overall risk management approach.

3.4 NIST Frameworks

Two NIST frameworks are discussed in the material: the Risk Management Framework and the Cybersecurity Framework.

Framework	Main purpose	Application
NIST Risk Management Framework (RMF)	Integrates security and risk management into the system development and operational lifecycle.	Used by U.S. federal agencies and applicable federal systems.
NIST Cybersecurity Framework (CSF)	Provides guidance for understanding, assessing, prioritizing, and improving cybersecurity risk management.	Can be used by organizations across different sectors.

Important distinction:

* NIST RMF focuses on a structured risk management process for information systems.
* NIST CSF provides an organizational framework for managing and improving cybersecurity risk.

The training material describes RMF as mandatory for U.S. federal systems and CSF as optional guidance for commercial organizations. Applicability of specific requirements depends on the relevant legal, contractual, and regulatory context.

3.5 ISO/IEC 27001:2022

ISO/IEC 27001:2022 is an international standard specifying requirements for establishing, implementing, maintaining, and continually improving an Information Security Management System (ISMS).

An ISMS provides a systematic approach to managing information security.

Its purpose includes:

* Identifying information security risks.
* Selecting appropriate risk treatment measures.
* Establishing policies and procedures.
* Assigning responsibilities.
* Monitoring security performance.
* Continually improving the management system.

The standard follows a management-system approach rather than focusing exclusively on individual technical controls.

Exam focus: ISO/IEC 27001 is associated with the requirements for an ISMS and continual improvement.

3.6 Framework Comparison

Framework or standard	Primary focus
COBIT	Governance and management of enterprise information and technology
BMIS	Holistic business and information security model
Balanced Scorecard	Measuring organizational performance from four perspectives
TOGAF / Zachman	Enterprise architecture
COSO ERM	Enterprise-wide risk management
ISO 31000	Risk management principles and guidance
NIST RMF	Information system risk management lifecycle
NIST CSF	Cybersecurity risk management and improvement
ISO/IEC 27001	Information Security Management System requirements

Memory aid:

* COBIT = Governance.
* BMIS = Business and security relationships.
* Balanced Scorecard = Performance.
* TOGAF / Zachman = Architecture.
* COSO / ISO 31000 = Enterprise risk.

* NIST CSF = Cybersecurity improvement.
* ISO 27001 = ISMS.

4. Strategic Planning and Implementation

4.1 Workforce Composition and Personnel Security

A security strategy must account for the people responsible for implementing and maintaining security.

Personnel security aims to reduce risks associated with employees, contractors, and other individuals who have access to organizational information and systems.

Measures include:

* Background checks where appropriate and legally permitted.
* Security awareness and training.
* Role-based access control.
* Appropriate assignment and separation of duties.
* Monitoring and management of access.
* Data Loss Prevention (DLP).
* Cloud Access Security Broker (CASB) capabilities.

The principle of least privilege should guide access decisions: individuals should receive only the access necessary to perform their responsibilities.

Exam focus: People are a critical part of information security. Technical controls alone cannot eliminate insider risk.

4.2 Centralized vs. Decentralized Security

Organizations must decide how information security responsibilities and services should be structured.

Characteristic	Centralized security	Decentralized security
Structure	Security is coordinated through a central function.	Security responsibilities are distributed across business units.
Consistency	Generally provides greater standardization.	May result in inconsistent practices.
Resource efficiency	Can reduce duplication and improve resource utilization.	May duplicate tools, personnel, and effort.
Local adaptability	May be less responsive to local requirements.	Can better accommodate business-unit needs.
Response	Central coordination can support consistent enterprise-wide action.	Local teams may respond more quickly to specific local issues.
Main challenge	Balancing central standards with business-unit needs.	Maintaining consistency, oversight, and cost control.

Centralized security advantages:

* Consistent policies and standards.
* Easier enterprise-wide oversight.
* Reduced duplication.
* More efficient use of specialized resources.

Decentralized security advantages:

* Better understanding of local business requirements.
* Greater flexibility.
* Potentially faster responses to local issues.

Exam tip: The best structure depends on enterprise size, complexity, culture, and business requirements. Avoid assuming that one model is always superior.

4.3 Security Awareness and Education

Security awareness and education help employees understand their security responsibilities and recognize potential threats.

A successful program should be:

* Continuous rather than a one-time event.
* Relevant to employees’ roles.
* Supported by leadership.
* Measurable.
* Updated as threats and business requirements change.

Examples of training topics include:

* Phishing and social engineering.
* Password and authentication practices.
* Data classification and handling.
* Incident reporting.
* Acceptable use.
* Remote working security.

The objective is to reduce human-related vulnerabilities and establish a security-conscious organizational culture.

4.4 Assurance Provisions and Oversight

Assurance activities provide confidence that security policies, controls, and processes are operating as intended.

Internal assurance may be conducted through:

* Internal audit teams.
* Dedicated security assessment functions.
* Security personnel performing appropriate reviews.

External assurance may involve independent third-party auditors or assessors.

External assurance may be required or expected in regulated industries, depending on applicable requirements.

Internal assurance	External assurance
Conducted by internal organizational functions.	Conducted by independent external parties.
Supports ongoing monitoring and internal oversight.	Provides an independent perspective.
Can be closely aligned with internal operations.	Can provide additional credibility to stakeholders.

4.5 Compliance Enforcement

Compliance requires more than publishing policies. Organizations need mechanisms to ensure that requirements are followed.

Important measures include:

* Visible leadership commitment.
* Clear policies and standards.
* Defined responsibilities.
* Disciplinary mechanisms for violations.
* Procedures for reporting noncompliance.
* Self-reporting mechanisms.
* Monitoring and enforcement.

Exam focus: Leadership commitment is essential. Policies without accountability and enforcement are unlikely to produce consistent compliance.

4.6 Risk Assessment Inputs

Strategic planning and implementation should be informed by a broad understanding of organizational risk.

The training material identifies four important inputs:

Input	Purpose
Business Impact Analysis (BIA)	Identifies the consequences of disruption to critical business activities and helps establish recovery priorities.
Resource Dependency Analysis	Identifies the resources, systems, people, and services required to support business operations.
Threat Assessment	Identifies potential sources of harm and the threats facing the organization.
Vulnerability Assessment	Identifies weaknesses that could be exploited by threats.

These inputs help determine which security initiatives should receive priority.

For example, a critical business process that depends on a vulnerable application and an unavailable third-party service may require additional protection and resilience measures.

4.7 Insurance as a Risk Management Mechanism

Insurance can be used to transfer certain financial consequences of risk to an insurer.

It is particularly relevant when an organization wants to manage rare events with potentially significant financial impact.

The material identifies three types of coverage:

Type	Explanation
First-party coverage	Covers certain direct losses suffered by the insured organization.
Third-party liability coverage	Covers certain liabilities arising from claims made by other parties.
Fidelity bonds	Protect against specified losses caused by dishonest acts, such as employee theft.

Insurance does not eliminate the underlying risk or replace security controls.

Exam focus: Risk transfer through insurance addresses financial consequences. The organization still needs appropriate risk management and security measures.

4.8 Gap Analysis and Metrics

A gap analysis compares the organization’s current security state with its desired state.

It helps identify:

* Missing security capabilities.
* Weak or ineffective controls.
* Resource deficiencies.
* Process improvements.
* Required projects and investments.

The results can be used to develop an implementation roadmap.

Key Measurement Concepts

Measurement	Meaning	Example
KGI – Key Goal Indicator	Measures whether a desired goal or outcome has been achieved.	Percentage of critical systems meeting defined security objectives.
KPI – Key Performance Indicator	Measures performance or progress of an activity or process.	Percentage of employees completing security training.
KRI – Key Risk Indicator	Signals changes in risk exposure or the potential for adverse events.	Number of critical vulnerabilities remaining unresolved.
CSF – Critical Success Factor	A condition or capability essential to achieving an objective.	Executive support for the security strategy.

Important distinctions:

* KGI = Did we achieve the goal?
* KPI = How well are we performing?
* KRI = Is risk increasing or changing?
* CSF = What must be in place for success?

4.9 SWOT Analysis

SWOT analysis helps an organization evaluate its internal and external environment.

Component	Meaning	Security example
Strengths	Internal advantages.	Experienced security personnel and mature security processes.
Weaknesses	Internal limitations.	Inadequate monitoring or insufficient security funding.
Opportunities	External conditions that may be beneficial.	New technologies that improve security capabilities.
Threats	External conditions that may negatively affect the organization.	Cybercrime, regulatory changes, and emerging attack techniques.

SWOT helps connect information security planning with the organization’s broader business strategy.

4.10 CMMI – Capability Maturity Model Integration

CMMI provides a model for evaluating and improving process maturity.

The training material identifies five maturity stages:

Level	Name	Explanation
1	Initial	Processes are ad hoc, unpredictable, and reactive.
2	Repeatable	Processes are established sufficiently to produce more consistent and predictable results.
3	Defined	Processes are documented, standardized, and applied across the organization.
4	Managed	Processes are measured, monitored, and controlled using metrics.
5	Optimizing	Continuous improvement is embedded in the organization.

Understanding the Maturity Progression

* Initial: Work is largely reactive.
* Repeatable: Similar activities can be performed consistently.
* Defined: Standard processes are documented.
* Managed: Performance is measured and controlled.
* Optimizing: Processes are continually improved.

Exam tip: The highest maturity level is not simply having more documentation. It involves using measurement, feedback, and continuous improvement to improve performance.

5. Information Security Program Objectives

Once the strategy is translated into an operational information security program, the program must establish objectives and controls that protect organizational information.

A fundamental objective is protecting the CIA Triad.

5.1 Confidentiality

Confidentiality ensures that information is accessible only to authorized individuals, systems, or processes.

Examples of controls include:

* Access control.
* Authentication.
* Authorization.
* Encryption.
* Data classification.
* Data Loss Prevention.

Example: Only authorized HR personnel can access confidential employee records.

5.2 Integrity

Integrity ensures that information remains accurate, complete, and protected against unauthorized modification or destruction.

Examples of controls include:

* Hashing.
* Digital signatures.
* Change management.
* Access restrictions.
* Audit trails.
* Data validation.

Example: Financial transaction records must not be altered without proper authorization and traceability.

5.3 Availability

Availability ensures that authorized users can access information and systems when required.

Examples of controls include:

* Backups.
* Redundancy.
* Disaster recovery.
* Business continuity planning.
* Resilience measures.
* Capacity management.

Example: A banking service must remain accessible to customers during normal operating conditions and recover within defined business requirements after disruption.

5.4 CIA Triad Comparison

Principle	Main question	Primary concern
Confidentiality	Who can access the information?	Unauthorized disclosure
Integrity	Can the information be trusted?	Unauthorized or incorrect modification
Availability	Can authorized users access it when needed?	Disruption or loss of access

Memory aid:

* Confidentiality = Keep it secret.
* Integrity = Keep it accurate.
* Availability = Keep it accessible.

8. Common Exam Traps

1. Choosing technology before understanding business objectives.
    Security strategy must begin with business needs and the purpose of the program.
2. Confusing governance with management.
    Governance establishes direction and oversight; management implements and operates.
3. Treating the desired state as the roadmap.
    The desired state is the target; the roadmap is the path toward it.
4. Assuming centralized security is always better.
    Centralization improves consistency, but decentralized structures may better support local requirements.
5. Confusing KPIs and KRIs.
    KPIs measure performance; KRIs indicate risk exposure.
6. Assuming insurance eliminates risk.
    Insurance can transfer specified financial consequences, but the organization retains responsibility for managing the risk.
7. Treating compliance as a documentation exercise.
    Effective compliance requires leadership support, accountability, monitoring, and enforcement.
8. Focusing only on technology.
    BMIS highlights the importance of people, processes, organizational design, and technology.
9. Confusing the NIST frameworks.
    RMF is a system risk management process; CSF is a broader cybersecurity risk management framework.
10. Assuming a mature process is simply a documented process.
    Higher maturity includes measurement, control, and continuous improvement.

9. Practice Questions

Question 1

An organization is developing a new information security strategy. Senior management wants to begin by purchasing advanced security technologies. What should be established first?

A. The technical architecture of the security tools.
B. The goal of the strategy and the program it will enable.
C. The number of security employees required.
D. The implementation schedule for all proposed controls.

Correct answer: B

Explanation: Strategy development should begin by understanding its purpose and how it supports business objectives. Technology selection and implementation planning should follow from that direction.

Question 2

An organization wants to measure whether its information security processes are becoming more effective. Which metric is most directly associated with measuring performance?

A. KRI
B. KPI
C. KGI
D. CSF

Correct answer: B

Explanation: A Key Performance Indicator measures the performance or progress of an activity or process. KRIs monitor risk exposure, while KGIs measure goal achievement.

Question 3

A multinational organization has several business units with different operational requirements. Each unit wants flexibility in how it implements security controls. Which organizational model best supports this requirement?

A. Fully centralized security.
B. Decentralized security.
C. Elimination of the security function.
D. Outsourcing all security governance.

Correct answer: B

Explanation: Decentralized security can provide greater local adaptability and alignment with business-unit needs. The organization must still maintain appropriate enterprise-wide oversight and consistency.

Question 4

An organization wants to evaluate its security processes. Processes are documented and standardized across the enterprise, but performance is not yet consistently measured. Which CMMI maturity level best matches this description?

A. Initial
B. Repeatable
C. Defined
D. Managed

Correct answer: C

Explanation: Defined processes are formally documented and standardized. The Managed level adds measurement and control through metrics.

Question 5

A company purchases cyber insurance to reduce the financial impact of certain security incidents. Which statement best describes this action?

A. It eliminates the underlying information security risk.
B. It replaces the need for security controls.
C. It transfers specified financial consequences to an insurer.
D. It guarantees that business operations will remain available.

Correct answer: C

Explanation: Insurance is a risk transfer mechanism for specified financial losses or liabilities. It does not eliminate the underlying risk or replace security controls.

Question 6

Which framework is most closely associated with a holistic model involving organizational design and strategy, people, process, and technology?

A. COBIT
B. BMIS
C. Balanced Scorecard
D. NIST RMF

Correct answer: B

Explanation: BMIS considers the relationship between business and information security through four core elements and six dynamic forces.

Question 7

An organization compares its existing security capabilities with its target security state to identify missing controls and improvement opportunities. What is this activity called?

A. SWOT analysis
B. Gap analysis
C. Resource dependency analysis
D. Business impact analysis

Correct answer: B

Explanation: Gap analysis identifies the differences between the current state and desired state. Its results can support prioritization and roadmap development.

10. Final Takeaways

The central message of CISM Domain 1B is that information security strategy must be business-driven, risk-informed, measurable, and achievable.

Remember these core principles:

1. Start with the business goal, not the technology.
2. Align security objectives with enterprise strategy.
3. Understand information assets, their sensitivity, and their location.
4. Define a desired state and create a realistic roadmap.
5. Use governance frameworks and standards to guide security direction.
6. Select an organizational structure that balances consistency and business flexibility.
7. Use risk assessments, gap analysis, and performance metrics to guide priorities.
8. Establish assurance and compliance mechanisms.
9. Improve process maturity through measurement and continuous improvement.
10. Protect confidentiality, integrity, and availability through an effective security program.

The CISM mindset: The security strategy should enable the organization to achieve its objectives while managing information security risk within acceptable limits. The security manager must connect business needs, governance direction, risk, resources, and measurable outcomes.
